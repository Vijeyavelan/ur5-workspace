Setup

The cell, as you described it

  • UR5 CB3 (not e-Series) with a Robotiq 2F-85 and a wrist RealSense on a 3D-printed mount.
  • Two Intel RealSense cameras, model not known yet. D405, D435 and D455 have different fields of view.
  • Bambu Lab X1E with the AMS on top.
  • Everything on one 3 ft × 6 ft table, with temporary cardboard sheets as a clean background for the cameras.
  • Goal: pick printed parts from the printer and record demonstrations (wrist camera + third-person camera) to train a VLA model.

Still open on your side: pick parts in place or pull the plate out (you want to try both), part size (the study assumes generous clearance), the RealSense models, which VLA, and where picked parts go.

Results

What the study found

  1. Straight-down picks through the door don't fit

    With the gripper vertical, the wrist sits about 0.28 m above the fingertips. The opening's top bar is about 390 mm above the printer's base and its sill about 45 mm (both estimates), and the AMS blocks the lid. Picks only work with the gripper tilted steeply into the printer, fingers closing left-right, over the middle three columns of the plate. In both pick layouts every reachable point needs a 70° tilt from vertical. The back of the plate, under the parked toolhead, is out of reach.

    Steep grasps grip 4 cm above the plate, so they assume parts at least about 5 cm tall.

  2. Pulling the plate out is the robust option

    The arm swings the door 0→90° and slides the plate a full 32 cm out, all in one arm configuration. Moving the base 2.5 cm in any direction barely changes the result (worst score 0.985 out of 1).

  3. Top access doesn't help

    With the lid off and the AMS beside the printer, the gantry, toolhead and frame still block most of the opening: 16 % of plate points reachable.

  4. Bed park height matters

    Bed Z 100–200 works. At Z 250 the plate tab sits too close to the door sill for the lower finger. For the pull layout the score drops from 1.0 at Z 100 to 0.61 at Z 250. The end G-code sets this.

Why every task uses one arm configuration

A UR5 can reach most poses with its wrist or elbow flipped either way. If some demonstrations flip partway through and others don't, the VLA gets inconsistent motions to learn from. So the study rejects any motion that needs a flip, even when a flip would reach more of the plate.

Pick in place reaches slightly more of the plate (40 %) but can't pull the plate, and its score drops to 0.467 within 2.5 cm of the spot. Top access reaches 16 % of the plate with the lid off and isn't viable.

Plate points come from a 5 × 5 grid of grasp points on estimated geometry. Use them to compare layouts, not as final coverage. Score combines pick coverage, door swing, plate pull and distance from arm singularities, each 0 to 1 with weights that are a judgment call; "at ±2.5 cm" is the worst score found when the base moves 2.5 cm in each direction, so the chosen spot tolerates placement error. Door to 75°: in the pick layouts, the arm configuration used for the task stops at 75°. A different configuration reaches 90° (a wrist flip outside the printer), or a magnet can hold the door open.

How sensitive is the score to the base position?

Score for each base position the search tried, at the height and bed Z of each layout. White cells have no stored result for that position. The chosen spot is the one whose worst score within ±2.5 cm is highest, not necessarily the brightest cell.

Data collection

Third-person camera

About the study's own drawings. The camera spot drawn in the code's top-view drawings (*_table_layout.png) and scene-camera renders (*_scene_cam.png), and stored in layout.json, comes from the first camera search. That is camera A below, which loses the elbow from view most of the time. It is not the recommendation. The drawings on this page leave it out.

What each camera is for

Most VLAs shrink each camera image to about 224 × 224 before the model sees it. At that size a 30 mm printed part is:

Camera30 mm part at 224 px
third-person, any placement tried3–6 px
wrist camera, pre-grasp23 px
wrist camera, at grasp36 px

So the wrist camera carries the part detail, and the third-person camera carries the task state: door open or closed, plate in or out, where the arm is relative to the door frame, which step of the task it's on. Frame the third-person view around the arm and the opening, not the parts.

E: whole cell, base in view

Shows the full arm pose, which helps a model that gets no joint angles. OpenVLA, for example, takes one image and nothing else. It costs about 35 % of the pixels on the printer and plate.

F: elbow outward

Drops the base, which never moves and carries no information, and keeps everything that moves, including the forearm passing through the door, where clearance is tightest. Suits models that also take joint angles and the wrist image (π0-style). F is aimed 60 % at the door opening and 40 % at the elbow's average position.

Which one fits depends on the VLA you train, so this page doesn't pick. Both sit past the edge of a 3 ft table: use a clamp arm or a floor tripod. From every outside viewpoint the arm hides about 30 % of the plate while it works inside, because the door is the only view into the chamber; the wrist camera covers that.

Settle before mounting the camera

  • The VLA model. Its input size, whether it takes joint angles, and how many cameras it uses decide between E and F.
  • The drop-off bin. It's part of the task, so it must be in frame. Once the camera is fixed, so is the bin.
  • Cropping. If the training pipeline center-crops to a square, the sides of a 16:9 frame are lost. Frame for the square, or record at 640 × 480.
  • Fixed exposure and white balance. The dark chamber next to a bright table makes auto-exposure flicker between episodes.
  • A rigid mount. A wobbly or folding table shakes a camera mounted on it; mount the camera off the table, or brace the table. Calibrate the camera pose (an AprilTag works), save a reference image, and compare against it at the start of every session.

While recording

  • Keep the background and lighting identical in every episode. The model learns from whatever is in frame, cardboard included.
  • Treat the camera as fixed once mounted. Moving it later doesn't destroy anything, but a trained policy expects the exact viewpoint it was trained on, so a shift means collecting new data and fine-tuning.
  • Every episode starts after the print, with the door closed. The X1E locks its door while printing.

Caveats

Limits

  • This is a kinematics and clearance study, not a motion planner. Motions are checked at waypoints 1.5–8 cm apart, not continuously in between.
  • The printed parts and contact forces (peeling a part off the plate, flexing the plate) are not modeled.
  • The results will change once the printer is measured.
